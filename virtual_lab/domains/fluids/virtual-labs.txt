### The Core Claim: NC is Not Computation — It is Exemplar-Based Mimicry
 
#### 1. What Turing Computation Actually Requires
 
The Turing Machine is defined by a tuple (Q, Σ, Γ, δ, q₀, q_accept, q_reject) where the essential structure is:
 
 
-  
A **finite, discrete set of states** Q
 
 
-  
A **transition function** δ: Q × Γ → Q × Γ × {L, R}
 
 
-  
**Exact, deterministic state traversal** — each configuration uniquely determines the next
 
 

 
The word *state* here is doing irreplaceable philosophical work. A Turing computation *is*  a sequence of configurations — each a triple (current state, tape  content, head position) — and the meaning of the computation is **constituted entirely by the state sequence and the transition rules**. The computation doesn't *represent* or *resemble* those states. It **is** those states. There is no gap between the symbolic structure and what is actually happening.
 
This matters enormously: Turing computation is **self-certifying** in a very precise sense. If you are in state q_i and read symbol σ, you *necessarily* transition to q_j. The process is entirely transparent to itself in terms of its own structure.
 
#### 2. What the NC Actually Does
 
The NC (specifically NCCLIGen and NCGUIWorld) is a  diffusion transformer trained on I/O traces — screen pixel sequences  conditioned on instructions and user actions. What it learns is a  conditional distribution:
 
**P(next_frame | instruction, previous_frames, user_actions)**
 
This is:
 
 
-  
**Continuous**, not discrete — it operates on pixel arrays, not symbol configurations
 
 
-  
**Probabilistic**, not deterministic — sampling introduces irreducible stochasticity
 
 
-  
**Surface-level**, not semantic — it learns what the screen *looks like* after a command, not what the command *does*
 
 
-  
**Correlational**, not constitutive — its outputs correlate with computation but are not constituted by it
 
 

 
The NC doesn't know *why* typing `ls` in a  terminal produces a file listing. It has learned that frames matching a  terminal context followed by the token "ls" are statistically  associated with frames showing a file listing. It is not executing a  filesystem traversal; it is **generating a plausible next frame** given what plausible next frames look like in its training distribution.
 
This is not a minor implementation detail. It is the **central architectural fact** of the system.
 
#### 3. Your Sharpest Move: Exemplar, Not Prediction
 
Your terminological distinction is philosophically precise and worth unpacking carefully.
 
**"Prediction"** implies a generative model with some grasp of underlying causal dynamics. A physics simulator *predicts* trajectories because it encodes (some approximation of) the causal laws. Even a weather model *predicts*  by modeling fluid dynamics. Calling NC "prediction" suggests the model  has internalized the rules of computation and is extrapolating from them  — which would give it too much credit.
 
**"Exemplar"** (from Latin *exemplar*, a  pattern to be copied) is more precise. An exemplar is a concrete  instance that serves as a model for imitation — not understood, but  replicated. What the NC has done is:
 
 
1.  
Observed many **instances** of computers running (the I/O traces)
 
 
2.  
Learned to **reproduce** outputs consistent with those instances
 
 
3.  
Generalized across exemplars to cover novel inputs — but only  insofar as those novel inputs fall within the distributional vicinity of  seen exemplars
 
 

 
This is **mimicry from instances**, not rule-following from principles. The NC acts *in accordance with* computational rules (it produces outputs that look right) without *following* those rules (it has no mechanism that instantiates the transition function).
 
This maps directly onto Wittgenstein's distinction in the *Philosophical Investigations*: there is a crucial difference between **following a rule** and **acting in accordance with a rule**.  A parrot trained on arithmetic examples may reliably say "4" when asked  "what is 2+2?" but it is not following the rule of addition. The NC is  structurally analogous: its compliance with computational behavior is  accidental relative to the rule — contingent on training distribution  coverage — not necessary relative to the mechanism.
 
#### 4. The Trajectory: Exemplar → Prediction, and Why That Still Isn't Computation
 
Your point about the trajectory is astute. As these models scale and generalize, they will shift from *replicating specific seen exemplars* toward *predicting novel outputs*  — generalizing computational behavior to inputs not directly observed.  This is already partially happening: the models can handle novel GUI  states not verbatim in training.
 
But here is the crucial follow-up argument: **prediction of computation is not computation**.
 
Consider a lookup table that perfectly predicts all outputs of a Turing machine for all inputs up to length n. This table *computes* the same function as the TM over its domain, but it is not *executing*  the computation — it is consulting a precompiled record. The NC, even  when it successfully predicts the right output, is in an analogous  position: it is performing **function approximation over the input-output behavior of computation**, not instantiating the computation itself.
 
The signatures of this are precisely what the paper admits:
 
 
-  
**Symbolic instability**: exact arithmetic and symbol  tracking fail because those require exact state, which the NC's  probabilistic continuous manifold cannot provide
 
 
-  
**Lack of execution consistency**: the same instruction  can produce different frames across runs — which is incoherent for  computation (a deterministic TM always produces the same result)
 
 
-  
**Failure of routine reuse**: capabilities don't  persist as stably callable routines because there is no genuine module  boundary — just a statistical tendency
 
 

 
These failures are **not bugs to be fixed by more compute or better training data**.  They are direct consequences of the architectural fact that the NC has  no discrete state space. They are the empirical manifestation of the  absence of genuine computation.
 
#### 5. The Deeper Structural Point: Constitution vs. Correlation
 
The cleanest way to state your argument at full generality:
 
**Turing computation is constituted by state transitions.** The computation just *is*  the sequence of configurations. There is no further fact about "what is  really happening" beyond the state sequence — the state sequence is the  computation.
 
**NC output is correlated with state transitions but not constituted by them.**  The NC produces outputs that statistically resemble the outputs of  computation, but the underlying process — diffusion over a continuous  pixel latent space — has no structural relationship to the discrete  state transitions that give computation its meaning.
 
This is the difference between a **map** and a **territory**.  The NC is learning an increasingly detailed map of computational  appearance. But the territory — the actual execution of state  transitions — remains absent. Meta's paper, despite its ambition, is  actually proposing to replace computation with an increasingly  high-resolution map of computation. The map is not the territory, no  matter how detailed it becomes.
 
#### 6. A Possible Counterargument and Why It Fails
 
A functionalist might object: if the NC reliably produces the right outputs for the right inputs, then *by definition* it is computing those functions — computation is just the right input-output mapping, and substrate is irrelevant.
 
But this fails for two reasons:
 
First, **approximateness**. Computation  requires exactness — a system that produces the right answer 99.7% of  the time is not computing. Computation is not a matter of degree; it is a  categorical property. The NC's probabilistic nature disqualifies it  immediately on these grounds.
 
Second, **scope**. A system that computes  correctly only within the support of its training distribution is not a  general computer — it is a finite approximation to a computer, bounded  by what it has seen. The Church-Turing thesis applies to *all*  computable functions; a system limited by distributional coverage cannot  in principle compute all computable functions. The NC is not  Turing-complete; it is a learned finite automaton approximation bounded  by its training data.

Quantum computers probably do not compute non-computable  functions. ### The theoretical answer has a hard limit and a real opening
 
This question has a precise complexity-theoretic answer, and it splits into two cases that are structurally opposite.
 
**The hard limit.** Circuit complexity theory  says some functions provably require large circuits — there is no  shortcut, not because we haven't found one, but because none exists. The  clearest example is a cryptographic pseudorandom function: the entire  point of a well-constructed PRF is that no circuit substantially smaller  than "evaluate the full construction" can reproduce its outputs,  because if a shortcut existed, the function would not be a PRF. If a  neural network genuinely learned to approximate a function with that  kind of intrinsic complexity — dense, incompressible, no exploitable  structure — then no extraction procedure can find a smaller explicit  program, by the same argument that makes cryptography work. In this  regime, your proposal is not an engineering challenge to be solved with  better tooling. It is provably impossible for the relevant function.
 
**The real opening.** But most of what neural  networks are trained to do is not this. The compression-prediction  equivalence already in the paper (§6.6) says the training objective *is*  a description-length-minimization objective. A network that generalizes  well — rather than merely memorizing — has, by definition, found a  compressed representation of the structure in its training data. If that  compressed representation has low true complexity (a small causal-state  machine, a short closed-form expression, a simple algorithm), then a  smaller explicit program computing the same function should exist in  principle, and the network's trillions of parameters are, for that  specific learned function, a wasteful implementation of something  intrinsically much smaller.

### The Core Claim: NC is Not Computation — It is Exemplar-Based Mimicry
 
#### 1. What Turing Computation Actually Requires
 
The Turing Machine is defined by a tuple (Q, Σ, Γ, δ, q₀, q_accept, q_reject) where the essential structure is:
 
 
-  
A **finite, discrete set of states** Q
 
 
-  
A **transition function** δ: Q × Γ → Q × Γ × {L, R}
 
 
-  
**Exact, deterministic state traversal** — each configuration uniquely determines the next
 
 

 
The word *state* here is doing irreplaceable philosophical work. A Turing computation *is*  a sequence of configurations — each a triple (current state, tape  content, head position) — and the meaning of the computation is **constituted entirely by the state sequence and the transition rules**. The computation doesn't *represent* or *resemble* those states. It **is** those states. There is no gap between the symbolic structure and what is actually happening.
 
This matters enormously: Turing computation is **self-certifying** in a very precise sense. If you are in state q_i and read symbol σ, you *necessarily* transition to q_j. The process is entirely transparent to itself in terms of its own structure.
 
#### 2. What the NC Actually Does
 
The NC (specifically NCCLIGen and NCGUIWorld) is a  diffusion transformer trained on I/O traces — screen pixel sequences  conditioned on instructions and user actions. What it learns is a  conditional distribution:
 
**P(next_frame | instruction, previous_frames, user_actions)**
 
This is:
 
 
-  
**Continuous**, not discrete — it operates on pixel arrays, not symbol configurations
 
 
-  
**Probabilistic**, not deterministic — sampling introduces irreducible stochasticity
 
 
-  
**Surface-level**, not semantic — it learns what the screen *looks like* after a command, not what the command *does*
 
 
-  
**Correlational**, not constitutive — its outputs correlate with computation but are not constituted by it
 
 

 
The NC doesn't know *why* typing ls in a  terminal produces a file listing. This is not a minor implementation detail. It is the **central architectural fact** of the system.
 
#### 3. Your Sharpest Move: Exemplar, Not Prediction
 
Your terminological distinction is philosophically precise and worth unpacking carefully.
 
**"Prediction"** implies a generative model with some grasp of underlying causal dynamics. A physics simulator *predicts* trajectories because it encodes (some approximation of) the causal laws. Even a weather model *predicts*  by modeling fluid dynamics. Calling NC "prediction" suggests the model  has internalized the rules of computation and is extrapolating from them  — which would give it too much credit.
 
**"Exemplar"** (from Latin *exemplar*, a  pattern to be copied) is more precise. An exemplar is a concrete  instance that serves as a model for imitation — not understood, but  replicated. What the NC has done is:
 
 
1.  
Observed many **instances** of computers running (the I/O traces)
 
 
2.  
Learned to **reproduce** outputs consistent with those instances
 
 
3.  
Generalized across exemplars to cover novel inputs — but only  insofar as those novel inputs fall within the distributional vicinity of  seen exemplars
 
 

 
This is **mimicry from instances**, not rule-following from principles. The NC acts *in accordance with* computational rules (it produces outputs that look right) without *following* those rules (it has no mechanism that instantiates the transition function).
 
This maps directly onto Wittgenstein's distinction in the *Philosophical Investigations*: there is a crucial difference between **following a rule** and **acting in accordance with a rule**.  A parrot trained on arithmetic examples may reliably say "4" when asked  "what is 2+2?" but it is not following the rule of addition. The NC is  structurally analogous: its compliance with computational behavior is  accidental relative to the rule — contingent on training distribution  coverage — not necessary relative to the mechanism.

Your point about the trajectory is astute. The NC, even  when it successfully predicts the right output, is in an analogous  position: it is performing **function approximation over the input-output behavior of computation**, not instantiating the computation itself.

-  
**Symbolic instability**: exact arithmetic and symbol  tracking fail because those require exact state, which the NC's  probabilistic continuous manifold cannot provide
 
 
-

**Failure of routine reuse**: capabilities don't  persist as stably callable routines because there is no genuine module  boundary — just a statistical tendency
 
 

 
These failures are **not bugs to be fixed by more compute or better training data**.  They are direct consequences of the architectural fact that the NC has  no discrete state space. They are the empirical manifestation of the  absence of genuine computation.
 
#### 5. The Deeper Structural Point: Constitution vs. Correlation
 
The cleanest way to state your argument at full generality:
 
**Turing computation is constituted by state transitions.** The computation just *is*  the sequence of configurations. There is no further fact about "what is  really happening" beyond the state sequence — the state sequence is the  computation.
 
**NC output is correlated with state transitions but not constituted by them.**  The NC produces outputs that statistically resemble the outputs of  computation, but the underlying process — diffusion over a continuous  pixel latent space — has no structural relationship to the discrete  state transitions that give computation its meaning.
 
This is the difference between a **map** and a **territory**.  The NC is learning an increasingly detailed map of computational  appearance. But the territory — the actual execution of state  transitions — remains absent. Meta's paper, despite its ambition, is  actually proposing to replace computation with an increasingly  high-resolution map of computation. The map is not the territory, no  matter how detailed it becomes.
 
#### 6. A Possible Counterargument and Why It Fails
 
A functionalist might object: if the NC reliably produces the right outputs for the right inputs, then *by definition* it is computing those functions — computation is just the right input-output mapping, and substrate is irrelevant.
 
But this fails for two reasons:
 
First, **approximateness**. Computation  requires exactness — a system that produces the right answer 99.7% of  the time is not computing. Computation is not a matter of degree; it is a  categorical property. The NC's probabilistic nature disqualifies it  immediately on these grounds.
 
Second, **scope**. A system that computes  correctly only within the support of its training distribution is not a  general computer — it is a finite approximation to a computer, bounded  by what it has seen. The Church-Turing thesis applies to *all*  computable functions; a system limited by distributional coverage cannot  in principle compute all computable functions. The NC is not  Turing-complete; it is a learned finite automaton approximation bounded  by its training data.

1. First, "surrogate" needs a precise definition

A simulator and a surrogate are not necessarily opposites.

A simulator is any system that maps a specified initial condition and intervention into a future state or observation.

The important distinction is between:

You explicitly represent the mechanism:

where Fθ​ comes from equations, rules, numerical methods, physical assumptions, etc.

You instead learn an approximation:

The substantive distinction is:

Suppose you observe:

The model learns that whenever A appears, B tends to happen.

But your scientific question is:
P(B∣do(A)).

Those are not generally the same thing.

A virtual lab therefore requires interventionally informative training data.

This is a major distinction from conventional foundation-model training.

For language, enormous passive corpora are often useful.

For an experimental environment, you fundamentally need experience under actions.

This aligns with emerging world-model research: recent work explicitly argues that passive video observation is insufficient for robust physical causal understanding and explores training world models on robot interaction trajectories. The same work reports stochastic instability and physical hallucinations even after extensive interaction data.

That is an important warning.
10. The training data therefore has to be designed around interventions

A naive pipeline is:
collect everything→train model.

A better pipeline is:
model→identify uncertainty→select informative intervention→real experiment→update model.

This gives you active surrogate learning.

And this is already becoming a technical research direction. A 2026 Journal of Computational Physics paper studies active operator learning with predictive uncertainty specifically so uncertainty can guide new data acquisition.

So the virtual laboratory naturally wants to become a closed-loop system:
Surrogate↔Experiment​

rather than a model trained once and frozen.
11. There is an even deeper issue: counterfactual stability

Suppose your model has excellent one-step accuracy:
∥s^t+1​−st+1​∥≪1.

That does not imply:
∥s^t+1000​−st+1000​∥≪1.

Errors can compound or interact with the system's dynamics.

This is especially severe for chaotic systems.

And current scientific ML literature repeatedly encounters the related problem of extrapolation. Neural-operator studies show that models can suffer large errors outside training support, with reliable extrapolation often requiring additional physics or new observations.

So the scientific benchmark cannot be:

    "Does the model predict the next frame?"

It has to be something closer to:

    "How reliably can the model support a sequence of interventions before reality needs to be queried again?"

That is a very different metric.
12. I would introduce a new concept: validated experimental horizon

Imagine asking:

    How far can I explore this virtual laboratory before its predictions become insufficiently trustworthy?

Define something like:
Hϵ​=max{T:validated error over relevant interventions≤ϵ}.

Then a good virtual laboratory doesn't merely have low prediction error.

It has a large validated experimental horizon.

And that horizon could depend on:
Hϵ​=f(domain,intervention,state,uncertainty,training coverage).

This suggests a particularly powerful design:

the virtual lab itself knows where it stops trusting itself.
13. That leads to the right architecture

I don't think the final system should be:
giant neural network.

It should be something like:

                         REAL WORLD
                             │
                  experiments / observations
                             │
                             ▼

And I would add a second layer:
mechanistic constraints​

The learned surrogate should not have to rediscover everything.
14. This is where hybrid models beat pure neural models

Current system-identification research is increasingly emphasizing exactly this point: prediction error alone is not enough for downstream control; learned models benefit from structural properties such as conservation, symmetry, monotonicity, dissipativity and other physics/control constraints.

So the strongest virtual laboratory is probably:
mechanistic model+learned surrogate+experimental data​

rather than:
pixels→pixels.

Mechanistic knowledge gives you:

    invariants,

    conservation laws,

    known causal structure,

    constraints,

    extrapolative priors.

The surrogate gives you:

    unresolved effects,

    empirical regularities,

    complex high-dimensional interactions,

    speed,

    compression of enormous experimental experience.

Reality then serves as the ultimate validation mechanism.
15. This also means "virtual lab" shouldn't mean "synthetic data generator"

That distinction matters.

A weak virtual laboratory is:

    Generate plausible experimental-looking outputs.

A strong one is:

    Generate outputs that remain interventionally predictive under sequential experimental manipulation.

The difference is huge.

A generative model can produce a beautiful molecular trajectory that violates chemistry.

A virtual laboratory needs to tell you:

    "This intervention is predicted to produce X, with 95% confidence; however, this region is outside validated support, and the next physical experiment should be Y."

That is a scientific instrument.
16. The National Academies essentially points toward this limitation

The 2025 National Academies report on foundation models for scientific discovery makes an especially important distinction: existing verification, validation and uncertainty-quantification practices for traditional simulation do not simply transfer to foundation-model-based scientific systems. It recommends treating them as research assets for exploratory and surrogate modeling while developing dedicated assurance methods before high-consequence deployment.

This is exactly right.

A learned virtual world isn't trustworthy simply because:
test-set accuracy=99.9%.

The validation question is:
Does it remain correct under the interventions scientists will actually perform?

That is much harder.
17. But this does not kill the idea

Quite the opposite.

It tells us what the research program has to solve.

The fundamental problem becomes:
Learn a compact computational representation of experimental behavior​

such that it supports:
prediction+intervention+branching+uncertainty+continual updating.​

That is substantially more ambitious than today's surrogate models.
18. And I think the truly transformative application is AI scientists

This is the part I'd emphasize most.

A human scientist's experimental throughput is tiny relative to computational throughput.

Suppose an AI scientist can cheaply explore one million hypotheses in a learned laboratory:
H1​,…,H106​.

It doesn't need to physically test all million.

The virtual lab can identify:
H37​,H8172​,H492001​

as high-value candidates.

Then the physical laboratory tests perhaps 20.

Those observations update the surrogate.

Now another million virtual experiments become available.

This is:
millions of virtual experiments→dozens of physical experiments​

rather than:
dozens of physical experiments→human interpretation.​

That could fundamentally change the economics of scientific discovery.

The self-driving-lab literature is already pursuing the physical half of this loop.

Your idea is essentially to add a learned experimental universe between the physical experiments.
19. There is an even bigger implication

Once you have a high-fidelity virtual laboratory, you no longer have to use it only for optimization.

You can use it for scientific reasoning itself.

An AI could perform:
Hypothesis discrimination

"What observation would distinguish H1 from H2?"
Experimental design

"What intervention gives maximum information?"
Counterfactual investigation

"What would happen if parameter X were changed?"
Rare-event exploration

"What conditions might produce an unusual phenomenon?"
Mechanism discovery

"What latent variables best explain these trajectories?"
Experiment triage

"Which physical experiments are actually worth performing?"

That makes the virtual lab an epistemic engine, not merely a faster simulator.
20. There is already an embryonic example of this idea in agent research

DISCOVERYWORLD is a useful conceptual precursor. It creates a simulated scientific environment in which agents have to formulate hypotheses, conduct experiments, analyze outcomes and discover explanatory knowledge, rather than merely answer questions. Its creators found that strong existing agents struggled on many of its discovery tasks.

But DISCOVERYWORLD has explicitly programmed environments.

The thing you're proposing is more radical:
Don’t merely build the virtual world.​

Instead:
Learn the virtual world from reality.​

That is a much more interesting direction.
21. The deepest theoretical question

There is a beautiful question hiding underneath all of this:

    What is the minimum learned representation of a physical environment that preserves all experimentally relevant distinctions?

You don't need to recreate reality.

You need to construct a sufficient experimental state:
Zt​=f(X≤t​)

such that for the intervention class A of interest,
P(Yt+1:T​∣X≤t​,A)≈P(Yt+1:T​∣Zt​,A).

If such a representation exists and is substantially smaller/cheaper than a full mechanistic state description, then the learned surrogate has discovered something very important:

    an experimentally sufficient state space.

This gives the project a much deeper theoretical foundation than simply "LLMs can simulate laboratories."
22. But there is a genuine boundary

We should be ruthless about where this fails.

A surrogate cannot magically infer information that the data do not identify.

No-free-lunch results remind us that generalization requires assumptions or inductive bias; current scientific ML work likewise shows serious degradation under distribution shift and extrapolation.

There will be domains where:
required intervention space≫observed intervention space.

There will be chaotic domains where long-horizon prediction is intrinsically difficult.

There will be rare events with almost no training examples.

There will be phase transitions where tiny changes produce qualitatively new regimes.

There will be systems where causal structure is essential.

And there will be phenomena that have simply never been observed.

So:

    A virtual laboratory cannot replace reality.

Its purpose is to make reality queryable much more intelligently.

That distinction is crucial.
23. Therefore I would formulate the central thesis this way

Not:

    "Neural computers will replace conventional computers."

Not:

    "Neural simulation is not real simulation."

Not:

    "AI can hallucinate a laboratory."

But:

    The next generation of scientific computing may be built around learned experimental environments: models trained from physical and computational trajectories that support intervention, counterfactual branching, uncertainty-aware exploration, and continual validation against reality.

And the economic argument is:
expensive reality→expensive training→cheap virtual experimentation​

while the scientific argument is:
physical experiments→learned environment→millions of counterfactual experiments→selective physical validation​
24. Where Meta fits

This changes how I would interpret the Neural Computers paper.

I don't think the strongest importance of Neural Computers is that Meta has demonstrated some radically new form of computation.

They haven't demonstrated that.

Their current prototypes are explicitly limited and fragile.

But there is a potentially important primitive underneath the framing:

    A sufficiently capable model can learn the externally consequential dynamics of an environment from interaction traces rather than being explicitly programmed with that environment's structure.

Today the environment is:

terminal / GUI.

Tomorrow it could be:

robot.

Then:

factory.

Then:

laboratory.

And eventually:

scientific domain.

That progression is much more interesting to me than "the model becomes the computer."
25. My assessment after the research

I would assign the idea three different levels.
The weak claim

    Neural surrogates can accelerate scientific simulation.

Already established.

Neural operators and surrogate modeling have substantial literature demonstrating this, sometimes with dramatic speedups.
The medium claim

    Learned surrogates can become components of autonomous experimentation.

Already emerging strongly.

This is happening in self-driving laboratories, Bayesian optimization, active learning and materials discovery.
The strong claim

    A general-purpose learned environment can become the computational substrate in which AI scientists conduct vast numbers of validated virtual experiments, with physical experiments serving primarily to update and audit the surrogate.

This is not solved.

But I think this is the genuinely important research frontier.

And critically, it is not merely an extrapolation of "better prediction." It requires solving state representation, intervention modeling, causal identification, uncertainty, long-horizon stability, active experimental acquisition, continual updating, provenance, and validation as one integrated system.
The idea I would pursue

I would actually give the paradigm a name separate from "Neural Computer":
Learned Experimental Environments

or more provocatively:
Virtual Science

The central loop:
REALITY⟶experiments​LEARNED ENVIRONMENT⟶interventions​VIRTUAL SCIENCE⟶uncertainty​PHYSICAL EXPERIMENT​

The breakthrough isn't that the virtual world perfectly replaces reality.

It is that one physical experiment can teach the system enough that it can subsequently explore thousands, millions, or billions of related experiments computationally—while actively identifying where the virtual world has become unreliable.

That is the part that could plausibly usher in a genuinely new era of laboratories.

And there is a particularly interesting connection to Meta's result: the same transition that turns a computer from an externally executed program into a learned environment could, at a much larger scale, turn a scientific domain from something we explicitly simulate into something we learn and experimentally inhabit.

Like for example: demis example in the chat of fluid equation vs googles' model producing fluid behaviour through learning right?

Yes — that is the example I think gets at the idea much more cleanly, and I checked the actual Demis discussion because the distinction matters.

In his conversation with Lex Fridman, Demis explicitly contrasts conventional fluid simulation based on difficult equations such as Navier–Stokes with video models like Veo that can produce surprisingly convincing liquid behavior simply from having learned from videos. He points to clear liquids being squeezed through hydraulic presses and says these systems appear to be "reverse engineering" some structure of material behavior from observation. He speculates that reality may have lower-dimensional structure that learning systems can discover.

That is almost exactly the surrogate-vs-explicit-simulation distinction you're getting at.

But there is an important refinement.
The conventional route

For a fluid, we start with something like Navier–Stokes:
∂t∂u​+(u⋅∇)u=−ρ1​∇p+ν∇2u+f

and then numerically solve it.

The computer is explicitly implementing our mathematical account of the dynamics.

The chain is:
theory→equations→numerical solver→trajectory​

Google has also worked on exactly this traditional-to-learned transition. Its earlier work on PDE simulation demonstrated neural-network-based methods for Burgers' equation, while importantly retaining known physics in the solver rather than learning everything from scratch.
The learned route

The Veo-style system is fundamentally different in where the knowledge comes from.

You give it massive numbers of observations:
(O0​,O1​,…,OT​)

and perhaps actions/conditions,
(A0​,A1​,…,AT​),

and the model learns some implicit mapping:
P^(Ot+1​∣O≤t​,A≤t​).

Nobody explicitly programs the Navier–Stokes equations into the model.

Yet the model can produce behavior that looks remarkably consistent with fluid dynamics.

That is the interesting phenomenon.
But I would be careful about saying "Google's model learned fluid dynamics"

That statement is too strong given the evidence.

What we know from Demis's example is that a video model can generate visually convincing fluid behavior. He himself says "presumably" it is extracting some underlying structure about materials, and treats the lower-dimensional-manifold interpretation as a hypothesis.

A generated video can satisfy:
P(plausible frame)

without necessarily satisfying:
P(physically correct future∣do(A)).

That's the distinction we need to preserve.

A video model may have learned a visual surrogate of fluid behavior without having learned a reliable interventional fluid simulator.

Those are very different achievements.
And this is exactly where your virtual-lab idea starts

Suppose we go one step beyond Veo.

Instead of asking:

    "Can you generate a video of water behaving plausibly?"

we train:
initial conditions+interventions→future physical state​

Then ask:

    What happens if I double the pressure?

    What happens if I introduce a different viscosity?

    What happens if the container geometry changes?

    What happens if I perturb the initial state?

Now we're no longer interested in visual imitation.

We're trying to construct a learned environment.
The crucial distinction can be expressed in three levels
1. Exemplar

The model has seen examples of fluids and produces something resembling those examples.
examples→plausible behavior

This is close to what the Veo example establishes.
2. Surrogate

The model learns enough of the domain's latent structure to predict outcomes for novel conditions.
(x,a)→y^​

Now it becomes scientifically useful.
3. Virtual experimental environment

The model supports sequential interventions:
(st​,at​)→st+1​

and you can branch trajectories:
st​⟶a1​​st+1(1)​ st​⟶a2​​st+1(2)​ st​⟶a3​​st+1(3)​.

That is the thing I would call the virtual lab.
And now Demis's comment becomes much more profound

His provocative observation is essentially:

    Engineers spent huge amounts of effort explicitly implementing fluid/material behavior, while a learned video model appears to acquire substantial amounts of that behavior merely by watching the world.

If this can be pushed far beyond visual plausibility, there is a major possibility:
Maybe we don't need to explicitly formulate every useful approximation of reality.

We can learn the approximation directly from reality.

That changes the scientific-computing pipeline from:
understand→formalize→simulate​

toward:
observe→learn→intervene​

And that is a much more radical idea than "neural networks can accelerate PDE solvers."
This is also why I think the word "surrogate" is actually excellent

Because we don't need to pretend the neural system has discovered the fundamental laws.

It may instead discover:

    an empirically adequate substitute for the full simulator over the region of experimental interest.

Imagine Navier–Stokes gives you:
Fphysics​(s,a)

while the learned system gives you:
Fsurrogate​(s,a).

You don't care whether
Fsurrogate​

has the same internal representation as the equations.

You care whether:
Fsurrogate​(s,a)≈Freal​(s,a)

for the interventions you're actually going to perform.

That is the scientifically meaningful criterion.
And here is where I think the idea gets really big

Consider several generations:
Generation 1

Equations

Humans discover equations.
Generation 2

Numerical simulation

Computers execute those equations much faster than humans could.
Generation 3

Learned surrogates

Models learn to approximate expensive simulations or observations.
Generation 4

Learned environments

Models learn sequential intervention-response dynamics.
Generation 5

Virtual laboratories

AI scientists conduct millions of experiments in those environments.
Generation 6

Closed-loop science

Virtual experimentation chooses which physical experiments should actually be performed, and those experiments update the learned environment.
Reality→Learning→Virtual experiments→Physical validation→Learning​

That last step is where I think the really important possibility lies.
There is also an important reason this could beat explicit simulation

Suppose the underlying physical system has an enormous microscopic state.

A mechanistic simulator attempts to approximate enough of that state to calculate the future.

But perhaps the experimentally relevant behavior lies on a much lower-dimensional manifold.

Demis explicitly speculates about precisely this possibility in the fluid example.

If
X∈RN

describes the enormous microscopic world, but experimentally relevant behavior lies approximately on
Z∈Rd,d≪N,

then a learned model might discover

That is potentially enormous.

Because you're no longer asking:

    "How do I solve the full equations faster?"

You're asking:

    "What is the smallest representation of reality that is sufficient for the experiment?"

That is a much deeper research question.
But there is a devastating test the surrogate must pass

This is what separates a cool demo from a scientific breakthrough.

Suppose the model watches:

    10 million videos of fluids

    millions of laboratory measurements

    millions of simulations.

Then you ask it about an intervention it has never observed.

If it produces a beautiful plausible video, that proves almost nothing.

We need:
counterfactual validity​

The model must correctly answer:
P(Y∣do(A=a))

not merely:
P(Y∣A=a).

And it needs to remain reliable over sequences of interventions.

That means the real benchmark for a virtual lab should eventually be something like:

    Can an AI conduct experiments in the surrogate and obtain conclusions that survive physical validation?

That's vastly harder than video generation.
